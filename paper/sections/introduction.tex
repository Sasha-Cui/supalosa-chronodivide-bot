\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Real-time strategy (RTS) games combine long horizons, partial observability,
large action spaces, adversarial interaction, and maps that change both
economic and tactical opportunities. These properties have motivated learning
environments and competitions ranging from microRTS to StarCraft
II~\cite{ontanon2018microrts,vinyals2017sc2le,samvelyan2019smac}. They also make
evaluation deceptively difficult. A bot can look stronger because it was tuned
on the reported maps, starts in the favorable physical slot, consumes a lucky
random stream, or is compared with a baseline loaded from the candidate's
modified dependency tree. Repeated matches do not repair such problems if map
families, randomness, and adaptation decisions are not the units of the design.

This paper studies that problem in Chrono Divide, a ground-up browser
reconstruction of \emph{Command \& Conquer: Red Alert 2} with an offline bot
API~\cite{chronodivide2026}. We configure StrongBot, an author-developed fork of
the public Supalosa scripted bot, and evaluate it against a clean pinned
Supalosa revision~\cite{supalosa2026bot}. Chrono Divide is an existing project,
not an environment introduced here. Our contribution is an auditable way to
use it for scripted-agent research and a bounded empirical case study of what
that process finds.

The repository initially contained many exploratory head-to-head runs and
parameter searches. A forensic reconstruction found that these artifacts were
not paper evidence: some used wall-clock-derived engine randomness, several
permitted exact-map behavior, maps were reused adaptively, source snapshots
were incomplete, and historical wins could not be tied to one clean baseline
and runtime. We therefore treated the existing bot as a starting point rather
than treating old performance as a result. The replacement design freezes
revision-aware map families, uses outcome-free compatibility screening,
creates family-disjoint roles before training, controls engine and
participant-specific randomness, evaluates reciprocal physical starts, and
fails closed on partial or selectively retried campaigns.

The final training pipeline performs five run-distinct deterministic searches
over 32 mutation-generated policies and a 28-field coordinate-free policy
space. Successive halving uses only training families. A common-seed
championship then compares all 30
run-finalist policies under shared family--seed blocks and selects one champion
without development or test access. A permissive signal gate on a fresh
development pool authorizes one frozen evaluation over \TestFamilyCount{} sealed test
families. Importantly, this pipeline was not the first development attempt: a
map-conditioned selector failed on an earlier development set and was retired.
The fixed generic-policy championship was specified afterward using training
evidence and a disjoint fresh development pool, while test identities and
outcomes remained sealed. We report this adaptation history rather than
presenting method v2 as if it had been the only idea tried.

This design separates two confirmatory questions from one post-confirmatory
diagnostic question:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item[RQ1.] Does the configured policy improve the shipped StrongBot default
  against pinned Supalosa on unseen map families?
  \item[RQ2.] Does the configured policy itself score reliably above 0.5
  against that opponent under the same family-level analysis?
  \item[RQ3.] Which paired outcome transitions and terminal-state patterns
  accompany any improvement, and do one-group policy reverts isolate a
  component? RQ3 is descriptive and cannot rescue either confirmatory gate.
\end{enumerate}

The principal result is positive but narrower than a claim of a winning bot.
Across \TestFamilyCount{} test families, the champion scores \ChampionScore{} and the shipped
StrongBot default scores \DefaultScore{} against the same Supalosa revision.
Their equally family-weighted difference is \ImprovementEstimate{} with a
family-clustered 95\% confidence interval
[\ImprovementCILower{},~\ImprovementCIUpper{}]. \PositiveFamilyCount{} family
effects are positive and \ZeroFamilyCount{} are zero. However, the champion
records only \ChampionWins{} wins in \ConfirmatoryGamesPerMethod{} games and
many matches reach the tick cap. Its
prespecified one-sided lower confidence bound for score above 0.5 is
\ChampionAbsoluteLower{}, so the experiment does \emph{not} establish that
StrongBot reliably beats Supalosa. The most faithful takeaway is that
configuration robustly improves a weak shipped default against this opponent,
mainly by turning losses into survival or wins.

Our contributions are threefold:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item We operationalize Chrono Divide as a deterministic, provenance-bound
  scripted-agent evaluation substrate, with revision-aware family roles,
  outcome-free compatibility checks, reciprocal starts, identity-keyed random
  streams, sealed outcomes, and fail-closed job accounting.
  \item We implement a reproducible training-only configuration pipeline:
  five run-distinct mutation-generated candidate pools screened by successive
  halving, followed by a common-seed championship over their finalists. The
  optimizer components are established techniques; their use is an engineering
  contribution, not a new general algorithm.
  \item We provide held-out evidence of a large, family-consistent improvement
  over StrongBot's default, report the failed absolute-strength endpoint, and
  triangulate the effect with paired transitions, terminal states,
  optimizer-selection diagnostics, and component reverts.
\end{enumerate}

The scope is deliberately finite: one independently authored opponent, an
Iraq mirror matchup, a pinned simulator, and a supported Temperate map-family
population. We make no claim about other opponents, factions, game versions,
RTS titles, ladder play, or human competition.
